# Reference library

Design traditions and exemplars to draw principles from. For each: what it
teaches, when to reach for it, and how borrowing it goes wrong. Pick one as the
main influence for a piece and at most one more as seasoning; mixing several
produces mush.

These are summaries of well-known approaches from general knowledge. Product
brands change their look over time, so borrow the reasoning, not a remembered
screenshot, and never reproduce another company's branding or assets.

## Choosing quickly

| The piece needs to feel | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| Clear, objective, information-first | Swiss / International Style |
| Geometric, confident, graphic | Bauhaus |
| Honest, minimal, long-lived | Dieter Rams |
| Premium, polished, trustworthy | Stripe |
| Fast, focused, tool-like | Linear |
| Simple, product-led, cinematic | Apple |
| Data-dense and truthful | Tufte |
| Authoritative, narrative, readable | Editorial (FT, NYT, The Economist) |
| Systematic across many pieces | Vignelli |

## Swiss / International Typographic Style

**Teaches:** the grid as the organizing principle; asymmetric layouts; flush
left, ragged right text; sans-serif type at few sizes with strong contrast;
whitespace as an active element; objectivity over ornament.

**Reach for it when:** the content is informational and should feel neutral and
rigorous. Reports, data summaries, technical decks, schedules, posters.

**Goes wrong when:** it becomes cold or empty because the hierarchy was not
actually resolved, or when the grid is treated as decoration instead of
structure.

## Bauhaus

**Teaches:** form follows function; primary geometry (circle, square,
triangle); limited palette, often primaries with black and white; type as a
graphic element; unity of art and utility.

**Reach for it when:** you want one strong graphic gesture, such as a cover, a
section divider, a poster, or a hero, with energy and confidence.

**Goes wrong when:** shapes are scattered as decoration with no function, or
the primary palette is used at full strength everywhere.

## Dieter Rams

**Teaches:** "less, but better". Good design is unobtrusive, honest,
thorough down to the last detail, and as little design as possible.

**Reach for it when:** building tools and interfaces that should disappear in
use, or when you are tempted to add something and need a reason not to.

**Goes wrong when:** minimalism removes things the user needed (labels,
affordances, contrast).

## Stripe

**Teaches:** meticulous polish in service of trust. Clear typographic
hierarchy, generous spacing, soft layered depth, a restrained palette with
confident accents, rich detail in small components, and documentation-grade
clarity for complex information.

**Reach for it when:** the piece must feel premium and credible: landing pages,
product overviews, investor or customer-facing decks, pricing.

**Goes wrong when:** you copy the gradient and skip the rigor. The polish reads
as premium only because the underlying layout and copy are precise.

## Linear

**Teaches:** speed and focus as an aesthetic. High density handled with strict
alignment, muted chrome, tight consistent spacing, subtle borders and surface
steps, minimal color with purposeful accents, keyboard-first clarity.

**Reach for it when:** building dashboards, internal tools, issue or status
views, and anything used repeatedly by people who want efficiency.

**Goes wrong when:** it turns into "dark mode with low-contrast gray text".
The real lesson is hierarchy within density, and it works equally in light
themes.

## Apple

**Teaches:** radical simplification. One message per screen, very large
confident type, product or key content as the hero, abundant empty space,
careful pacing from one beat to the next, consistency across everything.

**Reach for it when:** presenting a single idea with impact: keynote-style
decks, launch pages, hero sections, a one-number slide.

**Goes wrong when:** applied to content that needs density; a reference table
does not want one number per screen. Also fails without excellent imagery or a
strong number to carry the emptiness.

## Edward Tufte

**Teaches:** maximize the share of ink that shows data; remove chartjunk; small
multiples for comparison; sparklines; integrate words, numbers, and graphics;
show the data honestly, with context and scale.

**Reach for it when:** any chart, table, or analytical report.

**Goes wrong when:** minimalism strips needed context (axis labels, units,
baselines), leaving a beautiful chart nobody can read.

## Editorial (FT, NYT, The Economist)

**Teaches:** narrative hierarchy through headline, standfirst, body, and
caption; serif and sans pairings; charts with a headline that states the
finding and annotations that explain it; a distinctive but limited palette.

**Reach for it when:** reports, long-form documents, explanatory pages, and
data stories where the reader should be led through an argument.

**Goes wrong when:** editorial styling is laid over content with no argument.
The form implies a story; there must be one.

## Massimo Vignelli

**Teaches:** discipline through a system. A handful of typefaces, a strict
grid, a limited palette, applied consistently across every piece so the whole
feels inevitable.

**Reach for it when:** producing a set (a multi-slide deck, a report series, a
suite of pages) that must clearly belong together.

**Goes wrong when:** the system becomes so rigid that the one slide or page
needing different treatment does not get it.

## Using a reference well

1. Name the problem you share with the reference (e.g. "dense status data that
   must be scannable").
2. Extract two or three principles that address it.
3. Apply them with your own palette, type, and content.
4. Check: would someone say "that's a Stripe rip-off", or "that's well made"?
   Aim for the second.
